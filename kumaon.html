Discover Kumaon

The hills, the history, and the places worth visiting around Bairati — for anyone in the family who's never been, or wants to go back.

HISTORY

A short history of Kumaon

Kumaon's recorded history as a seat of power begins with the Katyuri dynasty, who ruled from around the 9th century CE, with their early capital at Joshimath and later centres across the region. Their era left behind an extraordinary number of temples — the Katyuris were prolific temple-builders, and clusters of their stonework survive across Almora district to this day.

By the 16th century, power passed to the Chand dynasty. King Kalyan Chand founded the town of Almora in 1568 as his capital, and it remained the political and cultural centre of Kumaon for over two centuries under Chand rule. In 1790, the Gorkhas of Nepal took control of the region; the British East India Company then took it from them in 1815, after the Anglo-Nepalese War, and Kumaon became part of British India's United Provinces.

Through all of this, Almora — and the hills around it, Bairati included — stayed culturally distinct: a Kumaoni-speaking world of terraced hill-farming, temple architecture, and the rice-paste aipan art still practiced today, largely untouched by the colonial hill-station building that reshaped places like Nainital and Shimla.

WHY BAIRATI

Why this corner of Kumaon matters

Bairati sits within Chaukhutiya (also spelled Chaukhutia) tehsil of Almora district — and Chaukhutiya itself turns out to have real archaeological weight. In a 2016–17 excavation, a group of small temples dating to around the 9th century CE was uncovered there, built in the same era as the Katyuri dynasty's rise. Some had lost their roofs to time, but the shivalingas inside were found still intact — evidence that this stretch of hillside was a place of settled, organised life a thousand years before anyone in this family tree was born.

Chaukhutiya is also a natural crossroads: roads from here run toward Ramnagar, toward Karanprayag, and toward Ranikhet and Almora — which is very likely why a family settled and stayed here across so many generations. It's not an isolated hamlet so much as a small point on old, well-used routes through the hills.

NEARBY

Places worth visiting from Bairati

All reachable on a day trip from the Chaukhutiya–Almora stretch.

Dwarahat

On the same road as Chaukhutiya

A town famous for its clusters of Katyuri-era stone temples — one of the richest concentrations of early medieval temple architecture in the region.

Ranikhet

~48 km from Almora

A hill station built around a cantonment, known for Himalayan views, a golf course, and a quieter pace than Nainital.

Someshwar

~35 km from Almora

An ancient Shiva temple built by Raja Som Chand of the Chand dynasty — a direct link to the same rulers who founded Almora.

Jageshwar

~36 km from Almora

A major Shiva temple complex with over 100 shrines dating from the 9th–13th centuries, set in dense deodar forest. One of Kumaon's most important pilgrimage sites.

Kasar Devi Temple

~8 km from Almora

A hilltop temple with a striking modern history — it became a gathering point for spiritual seekers in the 1960s–70s, visited by figures like Bob Dylan and Cat Stevens.

Binsar Wildlife Sanctuary

~30 km from Almora

A forested sanctuary with panoramic Himalayan views, home to leopards, barking deer, and the Himalayan monal.

Kainchi Dham

~45 km from Almora

An ashram on the Nainital–Almora road founded by Neem Karoli Baba in 1962, drawing large crowds for its annual June gathering.

Almora town itself

The district headquarters

Bright End Corner for sunrise/sunset views, the Nanda Devi temple, and Lal Bazaar for local crafts and woollens.
