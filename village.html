Bairati, Almora — the ancestral village

In the Kumaon Himalayas of Uttarakhand, once part of the Katyuri and later Chand kingdoms. Open in Google Maps · See it on the journeys map · See it in 3D · The family house in 3D

The region

Almora was the seat of the Katyuri dynasty from around the 9th century, then capital of the Chand kings from 1560 onward — the political and cultural heart of Kumaon for centuries. Unlike Shimla or Nainital, Almora was never built up by the British; it's a hill station shaped entirely by Kumaoni hands, known for its temples, its stone-and-wood architecture, and its folk music.

Bairati itself is small enough that it doesn't appear in indexed histories — which is normal for a hamlet, not a gap in research. What's known of it lives in the family's own memory, and in the land records below.

Aipan — the art of this land

Aipan is the ritual floor-and-wall art of Kumaon: geometric patterns — lotuses, chowkis, temple motifs, borders of triangles and dots — drawn freehand in rice-paste white (biswar) on a base of red ochre (geru). Traditionally made at thresholds, in worship spaces, and for festivals and ceremonies, it's one of the most recognisable visual traditions of this region. This site's colour palette and border patterns are drawn from it.
